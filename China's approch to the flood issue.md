## 1. Four-colour warning system

China uses a national colour-coded flood-weather warning system:

|Alert|Meaning|Typical response|
|---|---|---|
|Blue|Possible flood danger|Monitor conditions and prepare|
|Yellow|Significant danger|Increase monitoring and prepare evacuation measures|
|Orange|Serious danger|Activate stronger emergency measures and prepare/ begin evacuation|
|Red|Most severe danger|Emergency response and urgent evacuation may be required|

Red is the most severe level, followed by orange, yellow, and blue. China also has a separate **four-level emergency response system**, where Level I is the most urgent. The warning colour and emergency-response level are related but are not exactly the same thing.english.www.gov+1

## 2. County- and village-level flash-flood warnings

For flash floods, China has developed warning platforms that operate at several administrative levels:

- Central.
    
- Provincial.
    
- Municipal.
    
- County.
    
- Local village or small-catchment level.
    

The system uses rainfall stations, river or water-level gauges, hazard maps, population information, and local evacuation plans. In many small catchments, warnings are designed specifically around the lowest houses, local terrain, river channel, and estimated time before flooding reaches the settlement.link.springer

This is particularly relevant to Assam: a warning threshold should not be based only on the riverbank height. It should also consider the elevation of nearby houses, roads, shelters, embankments, and evacuation routes.

## 3. Rainfall-threshold warning

China uses rainfall thresholds for locations where water-level sensors are insufficient. The system can compare:

- Rainfall in the last hour.
    
- Rainfall over several hours.
    
- Cumulative rainfall.
    
- Antecedent rainfall from previous days.
    
- Rainfall intensity.
    
- Soil moisture or estimated soil saturation.
    

A useful method described in research on China’s system is a **compound warning index**, which combines rainfall intensity with effective cumulative rainfall. This is better than using only instantaneous rainfall or only the current river level.link.springer

For example, 50 mm of rain may not have the same effect when:

- The soil is dry and the river is low.
    
- The soil is already saturated after several days of rain.
    
- The ground is urbanized and cannot absorb water.
    
- The river is already near its danger level.
    

## 4. Dynamic hydrological warning

The more advanced approach uses real-time hydrological modelling. It estimates runoff, soil moisture, streamflow, and water-level development, then compares the forecast with local warning thresholds.

A simplified flow is:

text

`Rain gauges + radar + forecast rainfall              ↓       Runoff estimation             ↓ River-level/streamflow forecast             ↓ Compare with local danger threshold             ↓ Warning level + evacuation recommendation`

China’s research and development direction includes dynamic warning indexes that combine automatic monitoring, rainfall data, soil moisture, distributed hydrological models, and water-level thresholds.link.springer

## 5. Local warning and meteorological warning

China’s flash-flood approach can be divided into three broad warning modes:

1. **Platform warning**: a central or regional system processes sensor and model data.
    
2. **Local warning**: local authorities or village systems issue warnings based on local gauges, sirens, loudspeakers, or field observations.
    
3. **Meteorological warning**: forecast rainfall and weather information indicate that flash flooding is likely before the river rises.
    

This combination is useful because a sensor may fail or may not provide enough lead time. A rainfall forecast can provide early warning, while a local water-level sensor can confirm that flooding is actually developing.
