## Consensus Findings

- **Monsoon rainfall is the ultimate trigger**, but all three sources agree it is not sufficient by itself to explain flood severity — geomorphology (Debbarma & Deen), terrain/land-cover susceptibility (Borah et al.), and structural/socio-economic vulnerability (Banerjee et al.) all mediate outcomes.
- **Low-lying, densely populated riverine areas bear disproportionate impact.** Debbarma & Deen point to the Brahmaputra/Barak valleys generally; Borah et al. pinpoint Silchar/Katigorah in Cachar specifically; Banerjee et al. identify Barpeta, Goalpara, Nalbari, etc. at the state scale.
- **Human activity amplifies natural hazard.** All three cite deforestation, land-use change, erosion, and unmanaged floodplain development as compounding factors.
- **Existing flood-control measures (embankments, river training) are inconsistently effective** and can create false security — implied in Debbarma & Deen's call for "modern technology," and explicitly demonstrated in Banerjee et al.'s "levee effect" findings.

## Contested / Divergent Points

- **Role of rainfall intensity vs. inundation duration:** Borah et al.'s susceptibility model treats rainfall as a primary weighted input (13% AHP weight, second only to TWI/distance-from-river at 14%). Banerjee et al., using _observed damage_ rather than _predicted susceptibility_, found rainfall's correlation with actual damage was weak and inconsistent (positive in 2019, negative in 2020) — arguing damage is better explained by inundation duration, embankment failure, and local factors than by rainfall volume itself. This is a genuine methodological divergence: susceptibility (hazard potential) vs. realized damage (actual outcome) are different constructs.
- **Wetlands/drainage:** Debbarma & Deen do not address wetland loss. Borah et al. treat water bodies/agricultural land as _higher_-risk LULC classes (more susceptible to flooding by virtue of proximity/inundation), whereas Banerjee et al. treat _swamp/wetland area_ as a protective buffer — declining swamp area correlates with _higher_ damage. These aren't strictly contradictory (a floodplain water body being inundated is different from a wetland's flood-buffering function) but the framings could confuse a reader skimming across papers.

## Gaps Across All Sources

- **No source models climate-change-driven changes in rainfall intensity/frequency going forward** in a quantitative way (Borah et al. cites INCCA 2030 projections but doesn't run scenario modeling).
- **Limited field validation.** Borah et al. explicitly note the absence of ground-truthing for their AHP susceptibility zones. Banerjee et al. field surveys are described as "explanatory case studies," not statistically representative.
- **Economic loss quantification is thin** — only Debbarma & Deen and Banerjee et al. mention rupee-figure damages (Banerjee et al. cites INR 10,000–15,000 crore/decade), and neither is rigorously modeled.
- **None of the three papers integrates upstream transboundary factors deeply** (e.g., Himalayan snowmelt, hydropower releases) except a brief mention in Banerjee et al. of the NEEPCO Ranganadi dam release exacerbating Lakhimpur flooding in 2023.

## Major Problems Identified

1. **Recurring, near-annual flooding** across Assam causing loss of life, displacement, and destroyed livelihoods (Debbarma & Deen; Banerjee et al.).
2. **Disproportionate damage to housing and displacement of populations** — the single largest components of realized flood damage (Banerjee et al.: housing ≈15–18%, displacement ≈11%, crops ≈10% of the damage index).
3. **Concentrated, high-population-density hotspots** (e.g., Silchar in Cachar; Barpeta, Goalpara, Nalbari at state level) face outsized risk because hazard and exposure overlap geographically.
4. **Structural flood defenses (embankments) failing or under-maintained**, producing a "levee effect" where communities are lulled into false security and suffer worse when breaches occur.
5. **Loss of natural flood buffers** — wetlands/swamps have shrunk dramatically (e.g., Korila Bil in Barpeta nearly vanished since 1955), reducing natural water storage capacity.
6. **Short but intense damage-escalation windows** (mid-July to mid-August) that current management approaches don't specifically target.

## Root Causes (Synthesized)

- **Meteorological/hydrological:** Heavy monsoon rainfall, intense short-duration rainfall events, upstream runoff from Himalayan catchments, high Topographic Wetness Index and drainage density in low-lying zones.
- **Geomorphological:** Low elevation, flat terrain, riverbank erosion along the Brahmaputra and Barak and their tributaries, high sediment load.
- **Land use / anthropogenic:** Deforestation, agricultural expansion into floodplains, unplanned urban growth increasing impervious surfaces (roads, built-up areas), wetland encroachment and drainage.
- **Infrastructural:** Poorly maintained or breached embankments (reportedly >80% of Assam's embankments lack timely repair per Banerjee et al.), inadequate drainage systems.
- **Socio-economic:** Lower per-capita income correlates with higher realized damage (weaker adaptive capacity — poorer housing, less preparedness, slower recovery); high population density in flood-prone zones increases exposure.
- **Institutional/governance:** Reactive, "flood-control-centric" and river-training-focused strategies rather than community-centered adaptation; disconnect between top-down infrastructure projects and grassroots needs (Banerjee et al.'s village-level field survey table documents this directly).

## Prevention / Mitigation Recommendations (Synthesized Across Sources)

|Strategy|Supporting Source(s)|
|---|---|
|Pre-monsoon embankment inspection, maintenance, and timely repair (targeting the mid-July–mid-August critical window)|Banerjee et al.|
|GIS-based flood susceptibility mapping to guide land-use planning and target high-risk zones (e.g., Silchar, Katigorah, Kalain in Cachar)|Borah et al.|
|Wetland/swamp restoration and enforcement of floodplain zoning to preserve natural water-retention buffers|Banerjee et al.|
|Improved drainage management and integration of Remote Sensing/GIS technology in disaster planning|Debbarma & Deen; Borah et al.|
|Community-centered adaptation: flood-resilient housing, raised platforms, indigenous early-warning practices, livelihood diversification, and integrating local/indigenous knowledge with technical planning|Banerjee et al. (field survey/Table 3 findings)|
|Socio-economic resilience building (income support, insurance, access to healthcare) to strengthen adaptive capacity in vulnerable districts|Banerjee et al.|
|Real-time flood simulation and early-warning systems tailored to local geography/timelines|Borah et al.; Banerjee et al.|
|Stronger institutional coordination and government implementation of disaster-management schemes|Debbarma & Deen|

## Notes

- Undated/imprecise figures in Debbarma & Deen (e.g., rounded population-affected numbers, some apparent typos in the source PDF such as "1954, 1962, 1972, 1977, 1984,1998,2002, 2004 and 2012") should be treated as approximate.
- Borah et al. and Banerjee et al. use overlapping geography (Cachar is one district within the state-wide scope of Banerjee et al.) but different units of analysis (susceptibility zones vs. realized damage), so their findings are complementary rather than directly comparable.
